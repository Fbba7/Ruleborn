What changed about the approach

Deviations from the plan the design document sets out, each with what it costs and why it was worth it.

D1

The build-willingness test runs first, not second

change Adopted
§26.7§13.3§30.8

§26.7 schedules the paper heist first and the build-willingness test after it. But the build test is ranked risk #1, and §26.7 itself calls it “the single decision that changes the most”. The heist test decides whether the game is fun; the build test decides what game is being built — authorship, authorship-with-scaffolding, or a pivot to generated islands the player curates.

→ Run both in parallel. Neither blocks the other, and the build test's answer changes what the rest of the plan is for.

D2

The paper prototype became a browser prototype

change Done
§19.27§26.7§25.5

Both pre-code gates as written assume about thirty people in a room — eight to twelve for the paper heist, fifteen to twenty for the build test. That cohort does not exist for this project. The questions did not change; the method did, because a link can be sent to strangers and a room cannot. §25.5 already budgets for recruited remote panels, so the route was costed.

→ Built and published. It also answers a question paper could not: whether a delegated crew feels like playing or watching (§30.7 premise 3), which cannot be tested on a board where a human moves the pieces.

D3

The prototype sits ahead of the determinism spike on the critical path

change Adopted
§26.7§24.2§26.5

§26.7 puts the determinism spike immediately after the paper tests. Because the paper test became software, a few working sessions of engineering now precede the spike — exactly what the paper test was designed to avoid.

→ Accepted: §26.5's own rule is that scope is the lever, and buying the two project-deciding answers cheaply is worth it. This does not relax the rule that gameplay code waits for the spike. The prototype is throwaway and written as such.

D4

A reconciliation step was inserted before the specification locks

change Open
§26.7

§26.7 goes spike → lock the simulation contract → lock detection → lock the law schema → lock saves. Every one of those assumes there is one specification to lock. Four systems currently have two (C1, C2, C3, C6), and a fifth has two budgets (C5).

→ Work the blocking half of this register before any lock. It is days of desk time and the cheapest work in the whole plan.

D5

The economy model was rebuilt from scratch

change Done
§24§10.2§14.2§14.3§9.3

§24 describes a Python model that generated roughly a quarter of the document's numbers. That model is not in the document, so none of those numbers could be re-derived or re-tuned (gap G7).

→ Rebuilt at tools/economy_model/. It reproduces the first month to within 10% and the Keeper curve to within three days, and it produced findings F1, F2 and F3.

D6

A launch-scope cut of roughly 40% is proposed

change Proposed
§26.1§26.5§26.8§30.8

§26.1's launch table asks for 48 laws, 144 creature forms, 44 resonances, 39 terrain features, 16 buildings, 27 screens and 12 languages, from one designer, one coding agent and three contractors. §26.5 says scope discipline is the main risk control and §30.8 ranks execution as risk #2 — but neither is applied to §26.1.

→ Proposed: 24 laws rather than 48 (the Codex already gates in chapters); 3 form-notes rather than 8; ~20 resonances rather than 44; 2–3 languages rather than 12 (§20.6 already says this); constellations to v1.1; wards at 2 levels rather than 5. The Island Workshop is the one thing not to cut — it is the only credible answer to the content treadmill at this team size.

D7

A risk was added: delegated control reading as watching

change Adopted
§30.7§30.8§19.4

§30.7 marks premise 3 — that delegated control still feels like agency — as only conditionally supported, names the failure mode (“my units are stupid”), and sets a 70% felt-control threshold. It does not appear in §30.8's ranked register. Meanwhile §19.4 concedes performative challenge is “almost absent by design”, and the system that decides it — pathfinding and crew control — is the largest specification gap (G1).

→ Added at rank ~4. The prototype measures it directly.

D8

Two risks were re-ranked

change Adopted
§30.8§19.4§20.10

Trust and safety moved from 6 to 8: the design has already engineered most of that risk away — no free text for minors, no uploads, structured data only, preset chat. Complexity overwhelming new players moved from 7 to 6: §19.4 calls it “the biggest risk in the whole design” and the four-interacting-rules cap is untested.

→ No action beyond ordering the mitigations accordingly.

D9

Iteration is given a budget

change Proposed
§26.8§19.17§26.5

§26.8 estimates ~270 agent runs (≈810 focused hours) and then states plainly that the iteration needed to find the fun is unbudgeted. For a novel genre blend with no reference implementation, that iteration is normally the majority of the work. §19.17 alone lists nine A/B experiments, each needing a playable build and a tuning pass.

→ Budget iteration at 1–1.5× the build estimate (a further 200–400 runs) and treat the total as the reference-class figure §26.5 asks for.

D10

The document should split into a specification and a research archive

change Proposed
§19§28§30

The document is a buildable spec and an evidence archive in one file, and §19, §28 and §30 are roughly 40% of the text. Both halves are valuable. Keeping them together means the spec cannot be read, diffed or implemented without the archive — and it is part of why four systems reached v2.3 specified twice.

→ docs/design/spec/ carries decisions, one file per system; docs/design/research/ carries why. Cross-reference, never duplicate. Started: spec/detection.md is the first file, and the twelve content tables in content/ are the data half of the same split.

D11

The prototype departs from the document's numbers on purpose

change Done
§12.6§12.8§12.17§5.4

Vault health is 600 rather than 1,500 so cracking takes about eight seconds; being caught takes 1.6 seconds of contact and takes one crew member at a time; there are two guards; the island is hand-built. The first build caught all three crew instantly, which read as unfair rather than tense and would have produced a false negative on gate 1.

→ Prototype-only. None of these numbers carries forward. The catch-takes-time change is worth considering for the real build, because instant total capture conflicts with §12.14's rule that failure stays temporary.

D12

change Adopted
§13.3§25.5

Server-side result collection would have been convenient, but declaring the database capability makes an artifact organisation-internal and unshareable publicly — which defeats the entire point of recruiting by link.

→ Results are collected in the browser and shown as a copyable code. tools/parse_gate_results.py scores them.

D13

The note distribution should be rebalanced before the law table is authored

change Proposed
§8.1§7.4§24

Parsed from all 48 law sheets: Glow is carried by 12 laws, its opposite Hush by 6. Hush is the hiding note — the raider-favouring one — and it is the scarcest, while the revealing note is the most abundant. Since laws are woven by the defender and apply symmetrically, defenders have both the least reason and the fewest options to weave Hush. Five of the twelve chords require it.

→ Rebalance toward roughly 8–9 laws per note. §24 freezes the note count at eight and makes laws the expansion axis, so this is a one-time cost now and a permanent constraint on every future season's six laws.

D15

Summary tables are now generated, not authored

change Done
§11.2§7.4§29.1§24.9

Four register items — C1, C3, C6 and C19 — have one cause: the same facts written down twice by hand, and one copy updated. The structural fix is that a table which exists only to summarise something else is never authored, only emitted.

→ tools/generate_docs.py emits §11.2's roster index, §7.4's law summary and §29.1's formulas appendix from content/. --check fails the build if a generated file has been hand-edited, and ./tools/check_all.sh runs it. This matters most for §29.1, which still carries the detection model §12.17 superseded — the worst place for a stale number, since it is where an implementer looks for it. It does not cover prose, which is what D10's split is for.

D14

Chords should require three laws, not three notes

change Proposed
§8.5

§8.5 calls chords “the late-game discovery” needing “three laws in play and a Lab of level 3”. Because 23 of the 48 laws carry two notes, all twelve chords are reachable with two laws, several with two commons. Slow rivers + Whispers gives Iron bell for 26 stability — inside a day-2 player's capacity of 51.

→ Require three distinct laws. It is a one-line rule change and it preserves the stated design intent; the alternatives are to reposition chords as a mid-game discovery, or to reduce two-note laws (which also helps D13).

D16

The Vault’s load rate is cut fivefold, from 150 a second to 30

change Proposed
§12.6§11.1§9.4

§12.6’s 150-loot-a-second is a published number and this changes it to 30 for Carriers and 8 for everyone else, with every role able to load rather than Carriers only. The reason is F5: at 150 a second the phase the document calls its central decision is two seconds long and has one option.

The replacement rate is not a guess. §12.6 sets its own target of one decision every 8–12 seconds, and a loading window shorter than one decision cannot contain a decision; 30 a second puts a full crew load at exactly ten. The rate scales with character level alongside Carry (§11.4), so the ten seconds holds for the whole game.

→ Accepted in docs/design/spec/vault.md, flagged for sign-off because it is a large move on a published figure. The only alternative that preserves the decision is raising carry capacity fivefold, which breaks §9.4’s exposure percentages and §11.1’s role balance instead. Doing neither means deleting the sentence that calls the Vault phase a decision.

D17

Ambush damage gains a floor, so the document’s own rule becomes true

change Proposed
§12.8§11.1

§12.8’s ambush is triple Power. F4 measures what that delivers: the same section’s claim that two ambush hits drop an equal-level guard is true for three of twenty-five pairings. This adds a floor — an ambush deals triple Power or half the target’s maximum health, whichever is greater — which changes a formula the document states in order to satisfy a rule the document also states.

→ Where two of the document’s own sentences cannot both hold, the one expressing intent wins over the one expressing a coefficient: “two ambush hits knock out a guard” is a design statement about how stealth pays off, and “triple damage” is a number chosen to deliver it. Half is the value that makes the intent exactly true; a third would need three ambushers, which a crew of three (§12.5, before Keeper 8) cannot spare, quietly deleting the tactic for early players.

Blocking — fix before any content or core code

Systems specified twice and incompatibly. Nothing downstream can be locked while two answers exist.

C1

Two incompatible detection models

conflict Proposed
§12.8§12.17§5.4§29.1

§12.17 specifies three vision zones inside a 30–45° cone plus peripheral vision, a 0–1000 suspicion meter, six guard states and noise travelling along walkable paths. §12.8 specifies one 120° cone at 6 tiles, awareness in percent per second, and two states. §12.17 presents itself as the fix, but §12.8 is left intact, §5.4's landing rule is written in the old percentage model, and §29.1, the formulas appendix, encodes the old model.

→ Drafted. docs/design/spec/detection.md states §12.17 once, with the four edits it forces spelled out: §12.8 becomes behavioural description only, §5.4's landing noise is restated as 250 of 1000 suspicion, §29.1 is regenerated, §21.12 is already correct. The numbers are data in content/detection.json and validated. Awaiting sign-off before the lock in §26.7.

C2

Two incompatible raid-payout models, differing by 16–30×

conflict Proposed
§9.4§9.6§12.1§12.9

§9.4 pays a percentage of what the defender holds; §9.6 replaces it with a haul minted from the raider's own level and says so explicitly — but §9.4's percentages and §12.9's multipliers remain. Run through the economy model, §9.4 delivers 32% of a player's income, reproducing its own stated “roughly a third” almost exactly, while §9.6 delivers 1–2%. Strong evidence that §9.4 generated the published progression tables and §9.6 was added afterwards without re-running them.

→ §9.6 is the better-reasoned model — minting is right when loot is created rather than transferred. Then: delete §9.4's percentages and §12.9's loot column; say what Carrier capacity now means (probably time pressure, not a cap on value — see G4); rewrite §9.4's and §12.1's resource-share claims; and resolve C4 first, since the formula's level term is undefined.

C3

Two law-rarity taxonomies, and one of them has no laws in it

conflict Open
§7.4§7.7§7.10§9.5§14.4§27.2§29.2

What is authored (§7.4, §7.7, verified by parsing all 48 law sheets): three ranks — 18 Common, 18 Rare, 12 Deep — at 15/30/60 pages. §14.4's 1,485-page total is computed from exactly that, and the glossary agrees. C4

The master inflation dial is keyed to a level range that does not exist

conflict Proposed
§5.2§9.4§9.6§13.4

§5.2's outer-realm table stops at level 5. §9.6's haul table quotes realm levels 2, 6 and 10, and attributes the 40%→71.5% sealed share to “realm level” — but §9.4 attributes that same share to the Vault, which does have ten levels. So §9.6 appears to have conflated the two and then built B(L) = 100 × 1.5^(L−1), which §9.6 itself calls “the master inflation lever”, on top of the conflation. At L=10 that is 25× its value at L=5.

→ Decide what L is — most likely Vault level or a composite target-strength score. If realms really cap at 5, the entire payout table is wrong by about 7×.

C5

Two defence budget systems, with three different ward limits

conflict Proposed
§5.2§5.5§13§13.4§10.2

§5.5 has one universe-wide stability pool from the Spire and Anchor Stones, with laws costing 10–26. §13.4 introduces a per-realm pool of 100/336/1,504 with laws at a flat 30/40. C6

Two ruleborn rosters — and eleven are different creatures, not different wordings

conflict Proposed

The same 18 creatures are specified twice. Prism Mite is a Carrier in §11.2 and a Scout in §11.11; Stormhound is a Scout then a Carrier; Rootrunner a Carrier then a Scout. C19

The production tables describe a universe the build rules do not permit

conflict Open

§9.3's published production figures fit a universe with one producer of each kind per realm, fully staffed. §5.2 permits two from realm level 3. And §11.5's roster ceiling of 38 characters cannot fully staff even the one-producer version, which needs 63 worker slots. All three tables cannot be correct at once. See findings F1 and F2.

G1

Pathfinding and crew movement

unspecified Proposed
§12.5§12.6§12.7§6§30.7

The document specifies in detail what guards perceive and what laws do to movement parameters, but never how a crew actually moves. Unanswered: is the world a tile grid or continuous (§6 is in tiles, §12.17's sub-metre zones and degrees-per-tick imply continuous); how does a player order movement at all (§12.7 covers abilities, never locomotion); what happens at an unplanned fork; how do 3–5 crew avoid each other; does the §12.5 route planner produce a literal path or waypoints re-pathed at runtime — which have very different determinism properties.

→ Drafted. docs/design/spec/movement.md, which unlike the other spec files is partly new design, since the document leaves this empty. It settles the world model first — tile topology, continuous position, which is the only reading that satisfies both §6's per-tile terrain and §12.17's sub-metre vision zones and degrees-per-tick turning — then proposes tap-to-move with per-member selection, deterministic breadth-first pathfinding with a fixed neighbour order, and commands rather than paths in the input log (§24.10).

The rule that protects premise 3 is stated explicitly: the crew never makes a decision the player did not make. No auto-attack, no auto-avoid, no auto-retreat, no re-routing around a guard the player walked them into. A unit cannot look stupid for a decision it never made. The prototype implements this and the felt-control question is measured directly against §30.8's 70% threshold.

G9

The 60-second guided build is the top risk's mitigation and is unspecified

unspecified Proposed
§13.1§27.2§16

§13.1 makes “a 60-second guided build before the first player heist” the primary mitigation for the design's number-one risk, and §27.2 confirms it as a decision. What three terrain features, which guard, which law, what the player is told, and — critically — whether the island they build then becomes their actual defence, are all unstated.

→ Drafted. docs/design/spec/first-build.md. The decision the sentence hides is whether the island becomes the player's actual defence — the document never says, and the two answers are different features. If it does not, this is a tutorial that is thrown away, and §49's finding is that people value what they built only when they finish it. The spec proposes it becomes the real defence, places it at the top of day 2 (after the player has met a cone from the outside, per §15.1's teach-in-context rule), and names the five placements that make a guard's cone mean something.

It also draws a distinction the document does not: the §13.3 test protocol and the shipped feature differ. The test tells the player they may stop, because stated willingness is worthless here. The shipped game says nothing and logs what they do. And it flags that §13.1's “not optional” sits oddly against §20.3's “no obligations” — reconcilable, since §20.3 is about recurring demands rather than onboarding, but the reason should be written down rather than assumed.

Design findings — these change how the game plays

Not clerical. Each is arithmetic from the document's own published tables, and each asks a question about the design rather than the prose.

F1

The roster cannot staff the universe the rules permit

finding Open
§5.2§10.2§11.5§9.3§4

Pure arithmetic from three published tables. A finished universe has 152 character positions — 126 producer worker slots (7 realms × 2 of each producer × 3 kinds × 3 slots), 21 guard posts, a crew of five — against a roster ceiling of 38 (3 at the Spire plus 7 Hearths × 5 homes). That is 25% coverage; the roster would need to be four times larger. Even production alone at one producer per realm needs 63 workers, before a single guard is posted.

→ This may be intentional — §4 names characters' time as one of two scarce resources. But three things follow that the document has not absorbed: §9.3's production figures are unstaffable; 25% coverage reads as “most of my buildings are idle” rather than as interesting scarcity; and the marginal Star Well becomes worthless, which removes a large part of §10.2's Starlight sink. Decide which lever moves: more Hearth homes, fewer permitted producers, or partial staffing made genuinely worthwhile.

F2

Building the second producer doubles income and breaks every pacing date

finding Open
§9.3§5.2§14.2§14.3

Solving backwards from §9.3's day-301 figure implies about seven fully staffed Star Wells at level 9 — one per realm, not the fourteen §5.2 allows. Running the economy model under all four readings, only “one producer per realm, no binding roster cap” tracks the document (117% of published at day 301; Keeper 40 on day 171 against a published 181). Every other reading is out by 2–4×.

→ A player who builds the second producer — a pure production gain, so everyone will — earns roughly twice what §14 predicts, and every “engaged player reaches this on day X” milestone becomes optimistic. Given F1, the second producer may be exactly the wrong thing to permit.

F3

The core trade-off the design is built on does not bind

finding Open
§4§11.6§12.6§12.9§9.6

§4 and §11.6 build the whole design on characters' time: “raiding more means earning less at home and defending worse”. A heist is 3 minutes and a clean escape rests the crew 45, so a crew of five raiding four times a day is withheld for 16 character-hours out of 744 — about 2% of production. With §9.6 returning 1–2%, the raid-versus-build tension is absent from the arithmetic entirely.

→ The scarcity the design needs already exists in F1 — it is simply not connected to raiding. Make crew membership an assignment that excludes work, the way guarding does, so five of 38 are committed rather than 0.67. The alternative is to lengthen rest substantially, which §12.14's temporary-failure principle argues against. Doing neither means dropping the claim in §4 and §11.6.

F4

§12.8’s knockout rule is true for three of twenty-five role pairings

finding Open
§12.8§11.1§11.4

“Two ambush hits knock out a guard of equal level” is the only statement in the document about how a fight ends. Read with the one cadence the document gives — §12.8’s own 0.8-second wind-up, one hit for damage equal to Power — it holds for three of twenty-five role pairings, or five under the most generous reading, and four of those five need a Breaker. A Mender attacking a Warden needs 68 hits, or 54 seconds, against a guard the ambush stunned for two. The rule requires Power = Health ÷ 4; the Breaker is 1.1× short and the other four roles are 2.5× to 5.4× short.

This is the same shape as C2 and F1: Power and Health were set for standing fights and home defence, where a Warden should take a long time to drop, and the ambush rule was written against them later without anyone multiplying the numbers together. python3 tools/decision_support.py combat.

→ Fixed in docs/design/spec/fights.md by giving the ambush a floor: triple Power or half the target’s maximum health, whichever is greater. Two ambushers then drop any equal-level guard in all 25 pairings — the stated rule, true by construction — while the Breaker’s one-shot on a Scout survives because the triple-Power term still wins there. The alternative, re-tuning Power or Health, moves home defence and every guard duel with it.

F5

The Vault’s loading phase lasts two seconds and 46% of crew capacity is unreachable

finding Open
§12.6§11.1§9.4

§12.6 budgets the Vault phase at about 30 seconds and names “when to stop loading” as its central decision. Its own numbers say otherwise. A Carrier holds 300 loot and loads 150 a second, so loading takes 2.0 seconds; and because §12.6 has only Carriers load, 260 of a five-crew’s 560 units of capacity cannot be filled by any means the document describes. The thirty seconds is cracking, and cracking is a progress bar with no decision in it.

The same arithmetic produced the one place the document’s numbers corroborate each other: cracking takes 10–25 seconds across the whole game against a phase length written in a different section from Vault health, which was written in a different section again from role Power. python3 tools/decision_support.py vault.

→ docs/design/spec/vault.md lets every role load, at a rate scaling with Carry, so the crew fills in ten seconds — one decision by §12.6’s own 8–12 second cadence. That is a fivefold cut to a published rate and should be flagged as such at sign-off; the alternative, raising carry capacity fivefold, breaks §9.4’s exposure percentages instead.

C20

The high revenue scenario quietly uses a different commission rate

conflict Proposed
§22.2§22.5

§22.5 says every figure in §22.2 assumes 15%. The low and base scenarios do — €2,720 and €25,500, both confirmed. The high scenario's €168,000 is computed at 30%. That is arguably correct, since €204,000 a month crosses Apple's $1M small-business threshold — but it contradicts the stated assumption and makes the three scenarios non-comparable.

→ Keep the 30% figure and footnote the threshold crossing. Worth surfacing that the discontinuity is a real business fact: revenue between roughly €70k and €85k a month is partly a dead zone.

G12

Three laws key off creature attributes that no creature has

unspecified Open
§7.4§7.3§11.1§11.11§11.10

Checked across all 48 law sheets against the role and creature tables. Density reads “Big characters get +30% health and −20% speed; small ones get +25% speed and −20% health” — but there is no size or mass attribute anywhere. §11.1's role table carries health, speed, power and carry, nothing else; §11.11's proportions are in heads, which is art direction for the character artist; §11.10's forms add quirks, never a size. A rare law's entire tier-1 effect has nothing to resolve against.

Heavy ground grounds “flyers”, and flight exists only in §11.2's Featherkin passive — so under §11.11 (the spec C6 recommends adopting) half the law is inert and its stated weakness is false. Swarm birth is milder: “two small guards with 30% of its strength” describes what it creates rather than an attribute it reads, but 30% of what is undefined, and split guards have no role, so §12.17 gives them no perception profile.

→ Either add the attributes (a size or mass band on each creature; flight as a stated property) or rewrite the three laws to key off role, which does exist. §7.3's rule is that laws change a realm's rule sheet and never contain special cases; a law reading a parameter that does not exist is the same failure from the other side. Enforce it in the law data table.

G2

What happens when a guard catches you

unspecified Proposed
§12.8§12.17§11.1§13

§12.8 gives ambush rules and a level cap, and §12.17 says an alerted guard converges and is never lethal. But there is no combat resolution: roles have Power (6–40) and Health (130–420) and attacks have a 0.8s wind-up, yet there is no attack rate, damage formula or range. Nor is it stated whether the crew can choose to fight, or what happens to loot carried by someone knocked out mid-run (§13 and §12.6 cover the clock-end and post-capture cases only).

→ Drafted. docs/design/spec/fights.md, which like movement.md is largely new design. It adopts the only cadence the document states — one hit per 0.8s for damage equal to Power — and that choice is corroborated rather than assumed: it is the reading under which Vault health, role Power and §12.6's stated phase length agree (see G4). It then answers the three open questions: the crew may fight at any time, but damaging a guard that has not been ambushed sends it straight to suspicion 1000, so an open fight is an alarm with a priced cost rather than a forbidden move; a downed crew member drops its loot on the tile and has ten seconds — one decision by §12.6's cadence — before capture, revivable by an adjacent ally in 3s, or 1.5s for a Mender.

It also repairs F4 by giving the ambush a floor of half the target’s maximum health, which makes §12.8’s own sentence true for all 25 pairings instead of three.

G4

The Vault's loading loop has no relationship to the payout

unspecified Proposed
§12.6§9.6§9.4

§12.6 gives Vault health, Breaker double damage, cracking noise and a 150-per-second load rate, and names “when to stop loading” as the Vault phase's core tension. But if §9.6's minted haul is authoritative (C2), loading longer earns nothing — so the phase's central decision currently has no mechanism behind it.

→ Drafted. docs/design/spec/vault.md. It reinstates the clause §9.6 deleted — §9.4’s “capped by the crew’s carrying capacity” — as a fill fraction on the formula in economy.md: what the crew banks is the raid’s full value times the share of its bags it filled. The faucet ceiling is untouched, so the inflation dial stays single, and loading longer earns more. It also prices two decisions the document names and never costed: dropping loot to run faster costs exactly the fraction dropped, and the stop-or-continue breakeven sits at 70% full — §12.9’s Loud multiplier, with everything else cancelling. Tuning the tension of the game’s emotional peak is then one number that already exists.

Decisions that are yours

These cannot be resolved by reading the document more carefully — they change what the game is. Each says what it blocks, what the numbers are, and a leaning where there is one. Q8 is small and comes first: it swings Q1 by a factor of three.

Q1

Is character scarcity a feature or an accident?

decision Open
§4§11.5§5.2§9.3

F1 shows 152 positions against a roster of 38. §4 claims characters' time is one of the game's two scarce resources, which would make this deliberate. But 25% coverage is not an allocation puzzle, and §9.3's production figures assume staffing the roster cannot supply.

→ Blocks: Q4, C19, the economy spec, and every pacing date in §14.
The numbers (decision_support.py staffing): 152 positions against a roster of 38; only 12 characters free to work once guards and a crew are committed; no second or third worker slot is ever worth filling, though §10.2 grants them at building levels 4 and 7; and two independent caps — Hearth homes and §9.5's birth curve — both say 30–40 without referring to each other.
Depends on Q8: if a Guardian Post holds more than one ruleborn, defence alone consumes 42–63 characters and the shortage stops being severe and becomes absolute.
No recommendation. This is a design choice about whether scarcity is the point. What is not a choice is leaving all three tables as they are.

Q2

Should the raid-versus-build trade-off be made real, or dropped?

decision Open
§4§11.6§12.9

F3 shows it is worth about 2% of production. It can be made real by making crew membership exclude work the way guarding does, or the claim can be removed from §4 and §11.6. What it cannot do is stay as written.

→ Blocks: nothing technical, but it decides whether §4's central claim survives.
The numbers: a crew of five raiding four times a day is withheld from work for 16 character-hours out of 744 — about 2% of production.
Leaning: make crew membership exclude work the way guarding does, so five of 38 are committed rather than 0.67. The scarcity needed already exists in F1; it is simply not connected to raiding. Lengthening rest instead runs against §12.14's rule that failure stays temporary.

Q3

Three law ranks or five?

decision Open
§7.4§7.10

C3. Both work; the reasoning in §7.10 survives either. Choosing five means re-tagging 14 laws and re-deriving the page economy.

→ Blocks: all law content authoring, and §14.4's page economy.
The numbers (decision_support.py rarity): five ranks completes the collection 23% cheaper — 2,460 pages against 2,805 — pulling it in about a month at eleven pages a day. §14.4's own 1,485-page figure is the three-rank total, so three ranks is what generated the published curve.
No recommendation. Five ranks needs fourteen Rares re-tagged, and the split is a judgement about complexity and excitement — §7.10 is explicit that rarity must not track power, which rules out the obvious cost-ordered answer.

Q4

One producer per realm, or two?

decision Open
§5.2§9.3

F2. Two doubles income against every published pacing date; one matches the tables but contradicts §5.2. Interacts with Q1.

→ Blocks: §9.3, §10.2, §14.2 and §14.3 — every published pacing date.
The numbers (F2): only “one producer per realm, no binding roster cap” reproduces the document (117% of published at day 301; Keeper 40 on day 171 against 181). Every other reading is out by 2–4×. A player who builds the second producer — a pure gain, so everyone will — earns roughly twice what §14 predicts.
Leaning: one per realm, given Q1. Under a roster that cannot staff what already exists, the second producer is not a decision, it is dead Starlight.
