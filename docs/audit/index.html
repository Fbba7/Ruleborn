What changed about the approach

Deviations from the plan the design document sets out, each with what it costs and why it was worth it.

D1

The build-willingness test runs first, not second

change Adopted
§26.7§13.3§30.8

§26.7 schedules the paper heist first and the build-willingness test after it. But the build test is ranked risk #1, and §26.7 itself calls it “the single decision that changes the most”. The heist test decides whether the game is fun; the build test decides what game is being built — authorship, authorship-with-scaffolding, or a pivot to generated islands the player curates.

→ Run both in parallel. Neither blocks the other, and the build test's answer changes what the rest of the plan is for.

D2

The paper prototype became a browser prototype

change Done
§19.27§26.7§25.5

Both pre-code gates as written assume about thirty people in a room — eight to twelve for the paper heist, fifteen to twenty for the build test. That cohort does not exist for this project. The questions did not change; the method did, because a link can be sent to strangers and a room cannot. §25.5 already budgets for recruited remote panels, so the route was costed.

→ Built and published. It also answers a question paper could not: whether a delegated crew feels like playing or watching (§30.7 premise 3), which cannot be tested on a board where a human moves the pieces.

D3

The prototype sits ahead of the determinism spike on the critical path

change Adopted
§26.7§24.2§26.5

§26.7 puts the determinism spike immediately after the paper tests. Because the paper test became software, a few working sessions of engineering now precede the spike — exactly what the paper test was designed to avoid.

→ Accepted: §26.5's own rule is that scope is the lever, and buying the two project-deciding answers cheaply is worth it. This does not relax the rule that gameplay code waits for the spike. The prototype is throwaway and written as such.

D4

A reconciliation step was inserted before the specification locks

change Open
§26.7

§26.7 goes spike → lock the simulation contract → lock detection → lock the law schema → lock saves. Every one of those assumes there is one specification to lock. Four systems currently have two (C1, C2, C3, C6), and a fifth has two budgets (C5).

→ Work the blocking half of this register before any lock. It is days of desk time and the cheapest work in the whole plan.

D5

The economy model was rebuilt from scratch

change Done
§24§10.2§14.2§14.3§9.3

§24 describes a Python model that generated roughly a quarter of the document's numbers. That model is not in the document, so none of those numbers could be re-derived or re-tuned (gap G7).

→ Rebuilt at tools/economy_model/. It reproduces the first month to within 10% and the Keeper curve to within three days, and it produced findings F1, F2 and F3.

D6

A launch-scope cut of roughly 40% is proposed

change Proposed
§26.1§26.5§26.8§30.8

§26.1's launch table asks for 48 laws, 144 creature forms, 44 resonances, 39 terrain features, 16 buildings, 27 screens and 12 languages, from one designer, one coding agent and three contractors. §26.5 says scope discipline is the main risk control and §30.8 ranks execution as risk #2 — but neither is applied to §26.1.

→ Proposed: 24 laws rather than 48 (the Codex already gates in chapters); 3 form-notes rather than 8; ~20 resonances rather than 44; 2–3 languages rather than 12 (§20.6 already says this); constellations to v1.1; wards at 2 levels rather than 5. The Island Workshop is the one thing not to cut — it is the only credible answer to the content treadmill at this team size.

D7

A risk was added: delegated control reading as watching

change Adopted
§30.7§30.8§19.4

§30.7 marks premise 3 — that delegated control still feels like agency — as only conditionally supported, names the failure mode (“my units are stupid”), and sets a 70% felt-control threshold. It does not appear in §30.8's ranked register. Meanwhile §19.4 concedes performative challenge is “almost absent by design”, and the system that decides it — pathfinding and crew control — is the largest specification gap (G1).

→ Added at rank ~4. The prototype measures it directly.

D8

Two risks were re-ranked

change Adopted
§30.8§19.4§20.10

Trust and safety moved from 6 to 8: the design has already engineered most of that risk away — no free text for minors, no uploads, structured data only, preset chat. Complexity overwhelming new players moved from 7 to 6: §19.4 calls it “the biggest risk in the whole design” and the four-interacting-rules cap is untested.

→ No action beyond ordering the mitigations accordingly.

D9

Iteration is given a budget

change Proposed
§26.8§19.17§26.5

§26.8 estimates ~270 agent runs (≈810 focused hours) and then states plainly that the iteration needed to find the fun is unbudgeted. For a novel genre blend with no reference implementation, that iteration is normally the majority of the work. §19.17 alone lists nine A/B experiments, each needing a playable build and a tuning pass.

→ Budget iteration at 1–1.5× the build estimate (a further 200–400 runs) and treat the total as the reference-class figure §26.5 asks for.

D10

The document should split into a specification and a research archive

change Proposed
§19§28§30

The document is a buildable spec and an evidence archive in one file, and §19, §28 and §30 are roughly 40% of the text. Both halves are valuable. Keeping them together means the spec cannot be read, diffed or implemented without the archive — and it is part of why four systems reached v2.3 specified twice.

→ docs/design/spec/ carries decisions, one file per system; docs/design/research/ carries why. Cross-reference, never duplicate.

D11

The prototype departs from the document's numbers on purpose

change Done
§12.6§12.8§12.17§5.4

Vault health is 600 rather than 1,500 so cracking takes about eight seconds; being caught takes 1.6 seconds of contact and takes one crew member at a time; there are two guards; the island is hand-built. The first build caught all three crew instantly, which read as unfair rather than tense and would have produced a false negative on gate 1.

→ Prototype-only. None of these numbers carries forward. The catch-takes-time change is worth considering for the real build, because instant total capture conflicts with §12.14's rule that failure stays temporary.

D12

The published prototype declares no runtime capabilities

change Adopted
§13.3§25.5

Server-side result collection would have been convenient, but declaring the database capability makes an artifact organisation-internal and unshareable publicly — which defeats the entire point of recruiting by link.

→ Results are collected in the browser and shown as a copyable code. tools/parse_gate_results.py scores them.

D13

The note distribution should be rebalanced before the law table is authored

change Proposed
§8.1§7.4§24

Parsed from all 48 law sheets: Glow is carried by 12 laws, its opposite Hush by 6. Hush is the hiding note — the raider-favouring one — and it is the scarcest, while the revealing note is the most abundant. Since laws are woven by the defender and apply symmetrically, defenders have both the least reason and the fewest options to weave Hush. Five of the twelve chords require it.

→ Rebalance toward roughly 8–9 laws per note. §24 freezes the note count at eight and makes laws the expansion axis, so this is a one-time cost now and a permanent constraint on every future season's six laws.

D14

Chords should require three laws, not three notes

change Proposed
§8.5

§8.5 calls chords “the late-game discovery” needing “three laws in play and a Lab of level 3”. Because 23 of the 48 laws carry two notes, all twelve chords are reachable with two laws, several with two commons. Slow rivers + Whispers gives Iron bell for 26 stability — inside a day-2 player's capacity of 51.

→ Require three distinct laws. It is a one-line rule change and it preserves the stated design intent; the alternatives are to reposition chords as a mid-game discovery, or to reduce two-note laws (which also helps D13).

Blocking — fix before any content or core code

Systems specified twice and incompatibly. Nothing downstream can be locked while two answers exist.

C1

Two incompatible detection models

conflict Open
§12.8§12.17§5.4§29.1

§12.17 specifies three vision zones inside a 30–45° cone plus peripheral vision, a 0–1000 suspicion meter, six guard states and noise travelling along walkable paths. §12.8 specifies one 120° cone at 6 tiles, awareness in percent per second, and two states. §12.17 presents itself as the fix, but §12.8 is left intact, §5.4's landing rule is written in the old percentage model, and §29.1, the formulas appendix, encodes the old model.

→ §12.17 is authoritative. Rewrite §12.8 as behavioural description only, restate §5.4's landing noise in suspicion units, and regenerate §29.1 from §12.17. Blocks guard AI, all stealth balance, and the HUD.

C2

Two incompatible raid-payout models, differing by 16–30×

conflict Open
§9.4§9.6§12.1§12.9

§9.4 pays a percentage of what the defender holds; §9.6 replaces it with a haul minted from the raider's own level and says so explicitly — but §9.4's percentages and §12.9's multipliers remain. Run through the economy model, §9.4 delivers 32% of a player's income, reproducing its own stated “roughly a third” almost exactly, while §9.6 delivers 1–2%. Strong evidence that §9.4 generated the published progression tables and §9.6 was added afterwards without re-running them.

→ §9.6 is the better-reasoned model — minting is right when loot is created rather than transferred. Then: delete §9.4's percentages and §12.9's loot column; say what Carrier capacity now means (probably time pressure, not a cap on value — see G4); rewrite §9.4's and §12.1's resource-share claims; and resolve C4 first, since the formula's level term is undefined.

C3

Two law-rarity taxonomies, and one of them has no laws in it

conflict Open
§7.4§7.7§7.10§9.5§14.4§27.2§29.2

What is authored (§7.4, §7.7, verified by parsing all 48 law sheets): three ranks — 18 Common, 18 Rare, 12 Deep — at 15/30/60 pages. §14.4's 1,485-page total is computed from exactly that, and the glossary agrees. What is described (§7.10, §27.2): five ranks — 18/14/10/6 plus 3 paradoxes — at 10/20/35/60/120 pages. No law anywhere in the document is tagged Uncommon. The two totals differ by 345 pages, which moves the whole late-game curve.

→ §7.10's reasoning survives either way — rarity should buy specificity, not strength. Decide the split, then regenerate §7.4, §7.7, §7.6, §9.5, §14.4 and the glossary from one table. Choosing five ranks means re-tagging 14 laws and re-deriving the page economy.

C4

The master inflation dial is keyed to a level range that does not exist

conflict Open
§5.2§9.4§9.6§13.4

§5.2's outer-realm table stops at level 5. §9.6's haul table quotes realm levels 2, 6 and 10, and attributes the 40%→71.5% sealed share to “realm level” — but §9.4 attributes that same share to the Vault, which does have ten levels. So §9.6 appears to have conflated the two and then built B(L) = 100 × 1.5^(L−1), which §9.6 itself calls “the master inflation lever”, on top of the conflation. At L=10 that is 25× its value at L=5.

→ Decide what L is — most likely Vault level or a composite target-strength score. If realms really cap at 5, the entire payout table is wrong by about 7×.

C5

Two defence budget systems, with three different ward limits

conflict Open
§5.2§5.5§13§13.4§10.2

§5.5 has one universe-wide stability pool from the Spire and Anchor Stones, with laws costing 10–26. §13.4 introduces a per-realm pool of 100/336/1,504 with laws at a flat 30/40. Ward limits appear three times: §5.2 and §10.2 say 4–8 per realm (3 + realm level), §13 says “three to seven”, §13.4 says 2–9. Guard posts: §5.2 says 1–3, §13.4 says 3–10 guards.

→ §13.4's mechanism is right — slot limits per layer are what enforce the “no layer above a quarter” rule, and cost alone cannot. Keep the limits, drop the second pool, price the layers in an explicitly named budget (parallel to terrain points), and reconcile the ward and guard numbers into one table in §5.2.

C6

Two ruleborn rosters, disagreeing on three roles and most abilities

conflict Open
§11.2§11.11§11.5§12.8

The same 18 creatures are specified twice. Prism Mite is a Carrier in §11.2 and a Scout in §11.11; Stormhound is a Scout then a Carrier; Rootrunner a Carrier then a Scout. That changes the role distribution (3 Scout / 4 Carrier versus 4 Scout / 3 Carrier), and role drives crew composition, guard behaviour (§12.8) and the Nursery's fewest-roles-first rule (§11.5). Most of the other fifteen disagree on ability, cooldown or resistance — Featherkin 12s or 14s, Echoplate returns at 50% or 40%, Rootrunner ignores Spores or Heavy ground.

→ §11.11 is the richer and later spec. Make it authoritative, reduce §11.2 to a generated index, and hold both in one creature data table — exactly the server-side content data §24 describes.

C19

The production tables describe a universe the build rules do not permit

conflict Open
§9.3§5.2§11.5§10.2§14.2§14.3

§9.3's published production figures fit a universe with one producer of each kind per realm, fully staffed. §5.2 permits two from realm level 3. And §11.5's roster ceiling of 38 characters cannot fully staff even the one-producer version, which needs 63 worker slots. All three tables cannot be correct at once. See findings F1 and F2.

→ Decide the building and roster rules, then regenerate §9.3, §10.2, §14.2 and §14.3 from the economy model rather than editing them by hand — hand-editing is what produced this.

G1

Pathfinding and crew movement

unspecified Open
§12.5§12.6§12.7§6§30.7

The document specifies in detail what guards perceive and what laws do to movement parameters, but never how a crew actually moves. Unanswered: is the world a tile grid or continuous (§6 is in tiles, §12.17's sub-metre zones and degrees-per-tick imply continuous); how does a player order movement at all (§12.7 covers abilities, never locomotion); what happens at an unplanned fork; how do 3–5 crew avoid each other; does the §12.5 route planner produce a literal path or waypoints re-pathed at runtime — which have very different determinism properties.

→ The largest gap, and the one most likely to fail §30.7's premise 3. Specify it before the vertical slice and make felt control a gate rather than a polish item. Pikmin is cited as the model in §2.2; make that concrete.

Design findings — these change how the game plays

Not clerical. Each is arithmetic from the document's own published tables, and each asks a question about the design rather than the prose.

F1

The roster cannot staff the universe the rules permit

finding Open
§5.2§10.2§11.5§9.3§4

Pure arithmetic from three published tables. A finished universe has 152 character positions — 126 producer worker slots (7 realms × 2 of each producer × 3 kinds × 3 slots), 21 guard posts, a crew of five — against a roster ceiling of 38 (3 at the Spire plus 7 Hearths × 5 homes). That is 25% coverage; the roster would need to be four times larger. Even production alone at one producer per realm needs 63 workers, before a single guard is posted.

→ This may be intentional — §4 names characters' time as one of two scarce resources. But three things follow that the document has not absorbed: §9.3's production figures are unstaffable; 25% coverage reads as “most of my buildings are idle” rather than as interesting scarcity; and the marginal Star Well becomes worthless, which removes a large part of §10.2's Starlight sink. Decide which lever moves: more Hearth homes, fewer permitted producers, or partial staffing made genuinely worthwhile.

F2

Building the second producer doubles income and breaks every pacing date

finding Open
§9.3§5.2§14.2§14.3

Solving backwards from §9.3's day-301 figure implies about seven fully staffed Star Wells at level 9 — one per realm, not the fourteen §5.2 allows. Running the economy model under all four readings, only “one producer per realm, no binding roster cap” tracks the document (117% of published at day 301; Keeper 40 on day 171 against a published 181). Every other reading is out by 2–4×.

→ A player who builds the second producer — a pure production gain, so everyone will — earns roughly twice what §14 predicts, and every “engaged player reaches this on day X” milestone becomes optimistic. Given F1, the second producer may be exactly the wrong thing to permit.

F3

The core trade-off the design is built on does not bind

finding Open
§4§11.6§12.6§12.9§9.6

§4 and §11.6 build the whole design on characters' time: “raiding more means earning less at home and defending worse”. A heist is 3 minutes and a clean escape rests the crew 45, so a crew of five raiding four times a day is withheld for 16 character-hours out of 744 — about 2% of production. With §9.6 returning 1–2%, the raid-versus-build tension is absent from the arithmetic entirely.

→ The scarcity the design needs already exists in F1 — it is simply not connected to raiding. Make crew membership an assignment that excludes work, the way guarding does, so five of 38 are committed rather than 0.67. The alternative is to lengthen rest substantially, which §12.14's temporary-failure principle argues against. Doing neither means dropping the claim in §4 and §11.6.

Significant — will cause balance or build errors

Smaller conflicts, but each one produces a wrong number or a wrong asset count if it reaches implementation.

C7

Tick rate: 20 Hz or 30 Hz?

conflict Open
§7.3§24§24.10

§7.3 and §24's engine table both say 20 steps a second. §24.10, the later simulation contract, says 30 and is self-consistent — it states a heist is 4,500–7,200 ticks, which is exactly 30 × 150–240 seconds. It also says “drop to 20” if a cheap phone cannot hold it, so 20 may be a pre-emptive fallback that leaked upward.

→ 30 Hz per §24.10; fix §7.3 and §24. Settle this before the golden replays are written, since they encode it and every duration expressed in ticks depends on it.

C8

The Library is load-bearing and specified nowhere

conflict Open
§7.6§12.1§10.2§26.1§30.15

“A Library at the Heart produces about one page per realm every few hours” is the mitigation for the problem review 10 identified — that making pages raid-only punishes the large share of players who prefer building. It is referenced four times. It is not among the 16 building types in §10.2, has no cost, no levels and no placement rule, and §26.1 confirms 16 types at launch.

→ Add it as a 17th building with a full level table, or fold the trickle into the Observatory or the Spire and rewrite the four references. Do not leave a load-bearing economic faucet unspecified.

C18

Aether storage: formula or table?

conflict Open
§9.1§10.2

§9.1 gives the Aether cap as 5,000 per Spire level plus 3,000. §10.2's Spire table gives a flat 5,000 × level — 5,000 at level 1, 50,000 at level 10. The formula would give 8,000 and 53,000.

→ Small, but Aether is the weaving and upkeep currency, so the cap gates how much law a player can re-weave in one session.

C20

The 48 law sound signatures are six school sounds repeated

conflict Open
§21.8§7.9§21.9

§21.8 says “every law also has a sound signature, so a player can hear which rules are running with the screen half-watched”. Extracting all 48 law sheets gives 6 distinct sounds, one per school, each repeated eight times — Motion laws all sound like “a rising whistle and a soft thump on landing”, Matter laws all like “a glassy chime and a low grind”. A player can hear which school is running, not which law, and a realm can hold several laws of one school. §21.9's asset list budgets 6 school stingers, so the budget matches the content and not the claim.

→ Either correct §21.8 to say sounds are per school — which is a reasonable design, and cheaper — or budget 48 law sounds in §21.9 and author them. The claim as written is not met by the content.

G2

unspecified Open

G10

Decisions that are yours

These cannot be resolved by reading the document more carefully. They change what the game is.

Q1

Is character scarcity a feature or an accident?

decision Open
§4§11.5§5.2§9.3

F1 shows 152 positions against a roster of 38. §4 claims characters' time is one of the game's two scarce resources, which would make this deliberate. But 25% coverage is not an allocation puzzle, and §9.3's production figures assume staffing the roster cannot supply.

→ Decide which lever moves: raise the roster, cut permitted buildings, or make partial staffing genuinely worthwhile.

Q2

Should the raid-versus-build trade-off be made real, or dropped?

decision Open
§4§11.6§12.9

F3 shows it is worth about 2% of production. It can be made real by making crew membership exclude work the way guarding does, or the claim can be removed from §4 and §11.6. What it cannot do is stay as written.

Q3

Three law ranks or five?
